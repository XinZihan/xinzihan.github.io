Online search engines, databases, and social media platforms organize information before users see it. Their rankings can be useful, but they should not define the entire research process.

This toolkit offers a five-step method for reducing dependence on one information system.

💬There is also an interactive checklist provided at the bottome of this page.✔

Remember, our goal is not to escape every algorithm. The goal is to recognize how search systems shape visibility and to create more than one pathway to knowledge.

Define the Need Expand the Language Search Across Systems Follow Community Pathways Compare and Reflect
Step 1

Define the Information Need

Broad searches give a ranking system more control over the results. You can begin by turning a general topic into a focused question.

For example:

Too broad Information about newcomers
More focused What employment services are available in Ontario for internationally trained workers?

A focused question identifies the location, audience, and type of information required.

Before searching, think 💭, and ask yourself:

  • What decision am I trying to make?
  • Whose perspective do I need?
  • Does the information need to be current or historical?
  • Does the answer need to be local or community-specific?
Step 2

Expand Your Search Language

The words you choose can change what you find. A community may use different language from a government agency, academic database, or library catalogue. You may miss useful sources when you search with only one phrase.

Type of language Example
Everyday language Help finding work
Institutional language Employment services
Policy language Labour-market integration
Community language Newcomer employment support
Related concept Foreign credential recognition

You could try several terms and compare the results. Older catalogue records may require historical terminology. You may use an older term to retrieve information without accepting or endorsing that language.

Create a search-term map Before you begin a detailed search, you could list community-preferred terms, institutional language, historical terms, related concepts, and alternative spellings.
Step 3

Search Across Different Systems

You may receive a wider range of information when you move beyond one platform. Different systems collect different materials and use different ranking rules.

You could compare a general search engine with a library catalogue, academic database, community organization, local news source, or government website.

For example,

"disability justice" housing Ontario
site:org "newcomer mental health" Canada
site:ca "Indigenous language revitalization"
"trans health" "community organization" Toronto
Tool How you may use it Example
Quotation marks You may search for an exact phrase. "disability justice"
site: You may limit results to one website or domain. site:org newcomer support
Minus sign You may remove an unwanted term. accessibility housing -hotel
Date filter You may limit results to a selected period. Past year or custom date range

Search features may change over time. You can treat these examples as starting points rather than fixed formulas.

Step 4

Follow Community Pathways

Once you locate a useful community source, follow the relationships around it instead of returning immediately to the main search engine.

Look for authors, partner organizations, recommended readings, archived materials, and cited reports. These connections may lead to sources that are not highly ranked by commercial systems.

Example pathway Community organization → local report → author biography → partner archive → additional community publication

Community-produced material should still be evaluated critically. No publication or organization represents every member of a community. Consider the source's purpose, audience, location, and limitations.

Greater visibility is also not always appropriate. Some knowledge may be private or culturally restricted. An Ethical searching requires attention to privacy and community control.

Step 5

Compare and Reflect

A critical search should compare more than one kind of authority. Try placing an institutional source beside a community-produced source. A scholarly or archival source can provide another perspective.

Source type Possible contribution
Government or institutional source Official policies, definitions, eligibility requirements, and program information
Community-produced source Local knowledge, lived experience, community language, and practical context
Academic source Research evidence, theoretical context, and broader analysis
Archive or historical collection Historical records, primary sources, and evidence of change over time

Authority depends on the question being asked. A government page may be authoritative about the wording of a policy. A community organization may better explain how the same policy affects people in practice.

Finish the search by asking what remains missing. Search results can reveal useful information, but their absences are also meaningful.
