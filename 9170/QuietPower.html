Quiet Power in Library Systems

Libraries organize knowledge through collection decisions, classification, subject headings, and metadata. These systems make discovery possible, but they also shape which materials users are likely to find.

A library does not need to remove a resource to limit its visibility. Material may be present but poorly described, placed in an unexpected category, or indexed through language that users do not recognize.

Click to read more about classification bias

Howard and Knowlton (2018) found that Library of Congress systems used for African American Studies and LGBTQIA Studies can disperse related materials across categories or describe them through outdated language. Browsing can therefore reproduce assumptions built into the classification structure.

Drabinski (2013) argues that classification systems are not neutral containers. Their categories reflect the historical conditions and institutional values in which they were created. As language changes, catalogue terms may no longer reflect how communities describe themselves.

Lo’s (2019) study of the Library of Congress heading “Illegal Aliens” shows how routine cataloguing language can carry political meaning. Users who reject or do not know an official term may still need to use it because relevant records were created under that heading.

Quiet Power and Hidden Knowledge

Search systems do more than find information. They decide what appears first, which language is recognized, and which sources receive attention. This influence is often difficult to see, so this project describes it as quiet power.

Information does not need to be removed to become invisible. It can be poorly described, ranked below popular sources, or left outside the system entirely.

Libraries organize knowledge through collections, classifications, subject headings, and metadata. These tools make discovery possible, but they also reflect historical and institutional values.

How Algorithms Shape Visibility🧐

Search engines and social media platforms rank information through automated systems. Their results may be influenced by popularity, engagement, advertising, and previous user behaviour.

In a study, Noble (2018) demonstrates that search results can reproduce existing racism and sexism. The first page is therefore not a complete view of available knowledge. It is a constructed selection shaped by social and commercial priorities.

How Knowledge Becomes Hidden

Marginalized knowledge may be difficult to find because it has not been collected, digitized, or preserved. Small community organizations may also have fewer resources to publish and promote their work.

Information can also be hidden by language. A community may use different terms from those used by libraries, governments, or databases. Relevant sources may exist, but users may not know the official or historical terms required to locate them.

Popularity-based ranking creates another barrier. Large institutions often appear before local organizations, even when community sources provide more relevant knowledge.

Hidden Does Not Mean Absent

Marginalized communities are not without knowledge. Their knowledge may exist in local media, community archives, newsletters, oral histories, or independent organizations.

Caswell et al. (2017) explain that community archives can support representational belonging. They allow communities to preserve their own histories instead of depending only on outside institutions.

The problem is often not the absence of knowledge, but the failure of dominant systems to recognize and prioritize it.

Why This Matters

The digital divide is not only about internet access or technical skills. It is also about whether users can reach information that reflects their lives and communities.

Libraries cannot remove every form of algorithmic influence. They can teach users to question rankings, search with different terms, compare platforms, and locate community-produced sources.

How Do We Respond to It?

We cannot completely remove algorithms or classification systems from information discovery. We can, however, reduce our dependence on a single system and make its influence more visible.

  1. Compare Different Information Systems

    Compare results across search engines, library catalogues, databases, and community websites. Visit our Search Toolkit for practical strategies.

  2. Change the Language of the Search

    Try community-preferred terms alongside institutional or historical language. Different search terms can reveal sources that one system may overlook.

  3. Include Community-Produced Sources

    Read community sources alongside government, academic, and mainstream materials. Comparing these perspectives helps reveal which voices are missing.

Think Beyond the First Page

  1. Who created the information system you are using?

  2. Why do some sources appear before others?

  3. Are you finding information produced by a community, or only information written about that community?

  4. What knowledge may remain outside the system?

The goal is not to find a perfectly neutral system. It is to search critically, follow multiple pathways, and question why some knowledge is easier to find than others.
